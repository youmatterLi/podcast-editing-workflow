# Episode Packaging: Hooks, Chapters, Progress Bar, Music, Subtitles, Name Tags

The numbers in this file (pause lengths, music levels, name-tag duration, character counts, etc.) are all **example settings, adjustable**, and don't represent a universal standard. The principles apply more broadly.

Audio-only works: progress-bar visuals, subtitle burn-in, name tags, watermarks, closing visuals and visual cards don't apply; hooks, chapter markers and music do.

## 1. Opening hooks

- **Number:** example 3–5 — more if each is short, fewer if each is long. What matters is whether they hook people, not how many there are.
- **Choosing them:** pick emotional high points and arrange them in an arc. Each must be **understandable on its own**: don't start mid-thought from a previous sentence or end half-finished. Hooks quote later content; that doesn't make them a separate chapter.
- **Pauses between hooks:** measured from where the previous sentence **really ends** (by audio energy, once trailing soft syllables and breath have decayed to the noise floor) to the start of the next. Can be classified by content, for example:
  - ordinary point, quick switch between short lines: about 0.3–0.5 s
  - big jump in content: about 0.5–0.7 s
  - emotionally heavy, needs a moment to sink in: about 0.6–0.8 s
  Keep trailing soft syllables, necessary breaths and meaningful emotional pauses (e.g. laughter); don't truncate the original audio to fit a gap length.
- **Sound inside the pause:** room tone from the same material at a matching level (if it's not long enough, crossfade a few short pieces together, avoiding a plain loop); **no digital silence**. With video, continue with the real footage after the previous sentence ends — no freeze frames, no black.
- **No music or transition sound effects under or between hooks** (example practice).
- Entry animation (optional): the same effect for each hook, faces must not look bad; fully gone before the next hook starts.
- After deleting, reordering or replacing a hook, recalculate the numbering, total, trigger times and pauses.

## 2. Chapters

Applies to: progress-bar chapters, on-screen chapter cards, the platform's chapter list. All three use the same set of titles, decided once.

### How to divide

- Understand each chapter from the **final kept content**: read a transcript that matches the output, and write a one-sentence internal summary per chapter to check your understanding.
- Start a new chapter where the question, the stage of the story, the subject under discussion or the direction of understanding changes substantively. Don't divide mechanically by equal length, by every question, or by keywords.
- Follow-up questions, explanations and responses within one story stay in the same chapter; brief digressions don't become chapters.
- **Start a chapter at the beginning of the question that opens that topic**, without splitting a question in two; align boundaries to the start of a sentence.
- Divide the full episode into a few large blocks (example: about 6). The interview outline is only a guide to granularity; follow how the content actually develops in the output.
- When used as a platform chapter list, meet the platform's format requirements (e.g. first entry at 0:00, a minimum number of chapters, a minimum length per chapter — check the platform's current guidance). The 0:00 chapter can cover the hooks and intro, with the first main chapter starting at the first sentence of the main content.

### Titles

- **Accurate:** represents the chapter's main thread; every core piece of information in the title can be found in that chapter's kept content.
- **Specific:** test — if it would also work for another episode, it's too vague. Avoid labels like "The importance of X", "About X", "Talking about X".
- **Engaging:** present a real question, contrast or change; suspense or a question is fine, but **don't make things up**.
- **Easy to read:** one main thread per chapter, no stitching; don't exceed the character limit you've measured for your layout — the limit is not a target. If it's too long, give the progress bar a short version, which may only be cut down or condensed from the full title.
- **Attribution and limits:** don't turn a personal experience into a general rule or a personal feeling into a scientific conclusion, and don't turn the host's view into the guest's conclusion; if the original said "maybe", the title doesn't say "definitely". Anything in quotation marks must be words actually kept. Don't infer what the guest was feeling inside.
- **Tone:** conversational, not preachy, no clickbait.
- Read each chapter title on its own: would someone who hasn't watched understand it? Does it contain references like "that night" or "that thing"?
- Don't reveal deleted content.
- Give **one** recommended title per chapter; only genuine ambiguity goes on the review list.
- Record titles the approver changed as "original → changed" with the reason, and read them before drafting titles next time.

## 3. Animated progress bar (not applicable to audio-only)

- **Applicability:** whether to add it, and above what length, is up to your show (example: added to longer landscape videos, not to vertical short clips).
- **Position:** along the bottom of the frame, not over people's heads, legible on a phone; not covering subtitles, name tags or faces.
- **Fills in real time with progress:** each section fills left to right within its own cell as it plays — not filled the moment the section starts, and not jumping cell to cell.
- **Restrained colour:** one accent colour is enough; use a low-key colour on long videos so it doesn't compete with subtitles for attention.
- **Display range** (example): not shown during hooks and intro, with progress counted from the first sentence of the main content; not shown during the outro; hidden while chapter cards are on screen.
- Chapter names don't overflow their cell; don't shrink the font to squeeze them in.
- **Checks:** start times strictly increasing, the first chapter starting at the start of the main content and the last ending at its end; take one frame either side of every chapter boundary to check progress; sample frames at the start, before and after each switch, and at the end. Legibility is confirmed by a person watching.
- Any re-edit means recalculating chapter starts and the progress bar.

## 4. Music

Principles:
- **Intro music comes in after the hooks** and is fully gone soon after the main content starts; it doesn't run on under the main content.
- **Outro music comes in under the last stretch of speech**, without covering the voice, and rises after the speaking ends; an outro track that's too short ends abruptly.
- **Intro and outro use different tracks** — identified by sound, not file name (another section cut from the same piece counts as the same track).
- **No music in the middle of the main content by default.** A transition track is used only at joins where a large deletion has left a big jump in topic; smooth joins get nothing. Within one episode use the same transition track with the same length and level, and not the intro or outro track.
- **Take a breath before a chapter card:** let the last word of the previous sentence finish completely and pause briefly before the card and music come in.
- Every music file needs a record of its source and licence.
- Audio sent for review is always taken from the **final mix**. You can't claim "it doesn't cover the voice" from gain or waveform; how it sounds is confirmed by a person.
- Entry points are recalculated from this episode's final output, not carried over from an old version's timecodes.

A set of example levels (relative to voice level, adjustable): intro fades in over about 0.3 s, fairly loud during the intro, drops when the main content starts and fades out within a few seconds; outro comes in at a low level under the last stretch of speech, rises within about 1 s around the end of speaking, and fades out after the whole track has played.

## 5. Intro and closing visuals

- **Intro:** leave a small breath between the intro and the main content (example: about 20 frames), using room tone.
- **Closing** (when there's video): once speaking is over and the outro music plays, avoid footage of people getting up and leaving; it can be replaced with a fixed end card that stays until the music ends.

## 6. Subtitles (not applicable to audio-only)

- **Source:** the finalised text, not the transcript. The transcript is only for locating and drafting.
- **Accuracy is a hard requirement:** cross-check two transcriptions against each other, settle mismatches by listening sentence by sentence, and check names and English words separately. If the subtitles contain something the original audio didn't say, don't synthesise audio to match the text.
- **Don't remove words actually present in the audio:** only fix punctuation and homophones, and add words that "the transcript missed but are in the audio"; it can be agreed to write stutters once and omit "um"/"ah".
- **Show only one line at a time**, with natural line breaks (example: at most 16 Chinese characters per line), with punctuation, following the audio: break first at real pauses in speech, then at punctuation, never splitting a word across two lines; short sentences aren't padded out; each line appears when speaking starts and disappears when it ends.
- **Both submission and checking take two passes:** ① against the audio: compare sentence by sentence with the audio; ② read for meaning: look specifically for homophone errors, pronoun errors (he/she), missing or extra characters — homophone errors are written the same way by the transcription and the subtitles, so pass ① can't catch them. What's submitted is **the version that will actually be burned in**.
- Program checks: order, start/end times, out-of-range times, unintended overlaps; search for prohibited content and its synonyms and common transcription variants. After burn-in, sample frames to check for truncation, occlusion and styling.
- When making bilingual subtitles for English content, cross-check with two transcription engines and then review each line by hand.

## 7. On-screen name tags (not applicable to audio-only)

- **Show only when the person is on camera**; in side-by-side framing each goes on their own side, in a single shot only one appears.
- Example duration: about 20 seconds.
- What it says is as specified by the host for that episode; **don't invent titles for guests**; refer to guests the way they themselves asked to be referred to.
- Keep text within the left and right safe margins; too close to the edge and the platform will crop it.

## 8. Watermark (optional; not applicable to audio-only)

- Whether to add one, and on which aspect ratio, follows your brand requirements.
- **Keep master and release versions separate:** first render a watermark-free master (revisions and short clips are sourced from it), then save the release version from the master, applying the watermark once at this step only. Check the source for a watermark before using it, to avoid stacking two.
- Don't put other graphics where the watermark is.

## 9. Visual cards (not applicable to audio-only)

- Use sparingly: only where something can't be remembered by listening and needs a visual; don't add a card for what speech already explains clearly.
- Delay the card: let the speaker say the point first, then show the card.
- Leave enough talking footage between cards, and keep chapter cards well apart from the content cards that follow.
- Changing text on visuals means **replacing, not adding**; read it back afterwards to confirm there's only one copy. Make one sample card for confirmation first, then do the batch.
